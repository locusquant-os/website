The maths behind the guardrails

Most AI systems ask you to trust them. We'd rather show you the arithmetic.

Here are six of the ideas the system is built on, with toy numbers so you can check them yourself. The production versions combine several of these and use parameters we don't publish.

01 · Uncertainty & sizing

Doubt costs more than it seems

When the system is less certain, it commits less, and it cuts faster than a straight line would. Position size falls with the square of confidence, not in proportion to it.

$$\text{size} = \text{size}_{\max} \times (1 - u)^2$$

where u is uncertainty, between 0 and 1. It combines several independent measures of how much the system doesn't know.

What this means for you: when the system is unsure, you are automatically less exposed, without anyone having to notice.

02 · Correlated agreement

Five analysts agreeing isn't five opinions

If your analysts all read the same news, their agreement is worth less than it looks. The effective number of independent opinions falls as their errors become correlated. The system measures how correlated its own AI analysts are and discounts their agreement accordingly.

$$N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{N}{1 + (N-1)\,\bar\rho}$$

where N is the number of analysts and ρ̄ is the average correlation between their errors.

1.47
effective independent opinions

Illustrative example: five analysts whose errors correlate at 0.6 are worth 1.47 independent opinions, not five.

What this means for you: a room full of AI agents agreeing does not fool the system into betting bigger.

03 · Calibration

Saying 80% should mean being right 80% of the time

A forecast is only useful if its confidence is honest. Group every forecast by the confidence it was made with, then check how often each group was right. A well-calibrated system sits on the diagonal.

$$\Pr(\text{correct} \mid \text{stated confidence} = p) \approx p$$

Illustrative, not results: 60% stated → 57% observed, 70% → 71%, 80% → 78%, 90% → 86%.

What this means for you: you can see whether the system's confidence deserves your trust, instead of taking it on faith.

04 · Honest scoring

Rewarding honesty about uncertainty

A good forecast is right and appropriately unsure. The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) grades both at once. Lower is better, and it punishes a confident wrong forecast hardest.

$$\text{CRPS}(F, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \big(F(x) - \mathbf{1}\{x \ge y\}\big)^2\,dx$$

where F is the forecast distribution and y is what actually happened.

Worked example: two forecasts both centred on +1%, one narrow (spread 1%) and one wide (spread 3%).

What happened Narrow forecast Wide forecast Which scored better
+1.2% 0.250 0.706 Narrow — it was right and said so
+4.0% 2.437 1.807 Wide — it admitted it might be wrong
−3.0% 3.436 2.562 Wide

What this means for you: the system is scored for being honest, not just for being bold, so it has no reason to overstate its confidence.

05 · Sourced evidence

If the source doesn't say it, it doesn't get in

This one is a rule rather than a formula. When the AI reads a filing or a news article and extracts a fact, it must point to the exact characters it read. Ordinary code, with no AI involved, checks that those characters really say what the AI claims.

$$\text{source}[\,\text{start} : \text{end}\,] \;=\; \text{quote}$$
Accepted The claim "revenue grew 12% year on year" points to a passage in the source that reads exactly that.
Rejected The claim points to a passage that says something else. It never enters the system, and no model is asked for a second opinion.

A second check confirms the passage actually supports the claim, not just that the words exist.

What this means for you: every fact behind a decision can be traced to the sentence it came from, and a made-up quote cannot get through.

06 · Human oversight

Measuring whether the human is really reviewing

"A human approves every decision" means nothing if the human has stopped reading. So the system occasionally shows reviewers a proposal with a known flaw planted in it, and measures how well they tell good proposals from bad ones. A raw catch rate is misleading, because someone who rejects everything catches every flaw. Signal detection theory separates real skill from caution:

$$d' = \Phi^{-1}(H) - \Phi^{-1}(F)$$

where H is the share of flawed proposals caught, F is the share of good proposals wrongly rejected, and Φ⁻¹ converts a rate to a standard score.

Worked example: two reviewers both catch 90% of flawed proposals. One also wrongly rejects 30% of good ones, the other only 5%. Their scores are 1.81 and 2.93: the second is genuinely better at seeing flaws, even though their catch rates are identical.

What this means for you: human oversight becomes a number you can track, not a sentence in a brochure.

07 · Optional

Knowing when to say "I don't know"

Every automated check in the system reports two numbers, not one: how often it declines to decide, and how often it is wrong when it does. For example: "declines on 12% of cases, and is wrong on 4% of the rest, against a 5% target." This lets you compare every safeguard on the same scale.

What this means for you: you always know how reliable each safeguard is, in plain terms.
